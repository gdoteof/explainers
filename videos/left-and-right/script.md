[fact: bienfait, archives]
From a room. In the summer of 1789, France's new National Assembly was meeting at Versailles, and arguing over how much power to leave the king: should he be able to veto its laws?

[fact: gauville, wilton | pause 0.9]
The deputies who stood by the king and the church took to sitting together, on the right of the president's chair. The ones who wanted bigger changes sat on the left.

> A card with the quotation, over the hall.

[fact: gauville | pause 0.4]
One of them, the Baron de Gauville, wrote it down in his journal: we were beginning to recognise one another. Those attached to their religion and to the king had stationed themselves on the president's right.

> The hall again. One dot, ringed, tries a seat here and a seat there, and ends up back on the right.

[fact: gauville | pause 0.8]
He had tried sitting in different parts of the hall, he wrote, to stay the master of his own opinion. It did not last. On the left he would always vote alone, and be jeered from the galleries. So he gave up the left side altogether.

## slow: Not official
> A page of the minutes. "The right side" is written in, struck out, and replaced.

[fact: gauville | pause 0.7]
Even so, the names were not official. The next April, Gauville asked for the minutes to name the right side, so that right and left would be told apart from then on. The Assembly ruled that the minutes would say only: a part of the Assembly.

## english: Into English
> Paris to London, and the first sighting. Then the manim chart.

[fact: wilton]
English got the words as news from France. The earliest known use is in a translation, printed in London in 1794.

[estimate: ngram]
And for a long time they stayed rare. Here is one way to see it: how often left-wing and right-wing turn up in English books.

[estimate: ngram | pause 1.3]
For more than a hundred years after that room: next to nothing. Then, in the nineteen twenties and thirties, they take off.
